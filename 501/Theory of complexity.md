The **theory of complexity** posits that how intricately a being is ordered limits both the [corpus](../030/Corpus.md) it can have and the [cerebrum](../030/Corpus.md#Corpus-corpus%20and%20corpus-cerebrum) that [cogito](../030/Cogito.md) can sustain. Complexity here is not a count of parts. It refers to how those parts are arranged, connected, and able to interact.    
  
Support for the theory comes largely from comparison. An electronic calculator consists of interconnected components arranged to perform arithmetic operations. Despite its capacity to process information, its structural and functional complexity is insufficient for it to be considered a corpus under this theory, and no cerebrum may form from it. An ant's anatomical structure features specialized systems packed into a microscopic frame, and its movement and behavior are evident, suggesting a degree of structural and functional complexity sufficient for corpus formation. The ant is consequently considered a more suitable candidate for corpus formation. The theory takes this to mean that arrangement, not size or material, determines what a corpus can support. No agreed unit of measurement for complexity exists, and the point at which a cerebrum becomes capable of thought has not been located with precision.  
  
According to the theory, thought does not arise from matter alone but from matter ordered sufficiently to support perception, pattern recognition, memory, and other forms of awareness. A corpus below that threshold may still function biologically or mechanically without giving rise to a higher cerebrum.  
  
## Formation of the constituents  
The theory holds that a being’s constituents do not form at once. Corpus comes first, as complexity organizes matter into a stable structure able to interact with its surroundings. Cogito follows as the cerebrum becomes able to process stimuli, form internal representations, and sustain awareness. [Anima](../030/Anima.md) is understood to form last, since it is built from the [experiences](../030/Experience.md) cogito gathers and interprets. It follows that no anima arises without cogito, and no cogito without a corpus of sufficient complexity.    
  
The threshold is not tied to a particular substrate. Neural tissue, distributed biological networks, synthetic processors, and armature have each been reported to reach it, and the theory does not rule out architectures not yet known.  
  
The theory therefore treats complexity as a condition of being: it is held to determine whether a corpus can give rise to thought, identity, and self-awareness, and the order in which those constituents form.
